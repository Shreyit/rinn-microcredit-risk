\subsection{Exercise 1: Dot product and linear score}
Given $\vect{w} = (1.5,\,-0.5,\,2)$ and $b=-2$. Predict class 1 (``likely to default'') if $s\geq 0$, otherwise class 0.

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{lccc}\toprule
Loan & $x_1$ (loan, INR ten-thousands) & $x_2$ (income, INR thousands) & $x_3$ (distance, km)\\\midrule
A & 3 & 4 & 2\\ B & 1 & 5 & 0\\ C & 2 & 6 & 1\\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}\end{table}

\question{E1.1}{1.5 marks}{Compute the score $s$ for A, B and C.}
Formula: $s = \vect{w}\cdot\vect{x} + b = w_1x_1 + w_2x_2 + w_3x_3 + b$.
\begin{align*}
s_A &= (1.5)(3) + (-0.5)(4) + (2)(2) - 2 = 4.5 - 2 + 4 - 2 = \finalans{4.5}\\
s_B &= (1.5)(1) + (-0.5)(5) + (2)(0) - 2 = 1.5 - 2.5 + 0 - 2 = \finalans{-3}\\
s_C &= (1.5)(2) + (-0.5)(6) + (2)(1) - 2 = 3 - 3 + 2 - 2 = \finalans{0}
\end{align*}

\question{E1.2}{1 mark}{State the predicted class for each loan.}
Rule: $\hat y = 1$ if $s \geq 0$, otherwise $\hat y = 0$.

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{lccl}\toprule
Loan & $s$ & Check & Predicted class\\\midrule
A & $4.5$ & $4.5 \geq 0$ & $\hat y_A = 1$ (likely to default)\\
B & $-3$ & $-3 < 0$ & $\hat y_B = 0$ (repaid)\\
C & $0$ & $0 \geq 0$ & $\hat y_C = 1$ (likely to default)\\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}\end{table}

\question{E1.3}{0.5 marks}{Which loan lies exactly on the decision boundary, and how do you know?}
\textbf{Loan C.} The decision boundary is the set of points where $\vect{w}\cdot\vect{x}+b = 0$,
and loan C has $s_C = 0$ exactly. It is classified as 1 only because the convention uses
$s \geq 0$.

\subsection{Exercise 2: A perceptron update}
Start from $\vect{w}^{(0)} = (1.5,\,-0.5,\,2)$ and $b^{(0)} = -2$. True labels: A $=0$, B $=1$, C $=1$. Learning rate $\eta = 0.5$, with
\[
  \vect{w} \leftarrow \vect{w} + \eta\,(y-\hat y)\,\vect{x}, \qquad b \leftarrow b + \eta\,(y-\hat y).
\]

\question{E2.1}{1 mark}{Which of A, B, C are misclassified by the starting $\vect{w}$ and $b$?}
Using the predictions from E1.2:

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{lccl}\toprule
Loan & True $y$ & Predicted $\hat y$ & Result\\\midrule
A & 0 & 1 & misclassified (false positive)\\
B & 1 & 0 & misclassified (false negative)\\
C & 1 & 1 & correct\\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}\end{table}

\finalans{\text{Loans A and B are misclassified.}}

\question{E2.2}{1.5 marks}{Process only loan A (a single update). Give the new $\vect{w}$ and $b$.}
For loan A: $\vect{x}_A = (3,\,4,\,2)$, $y_A = 0$, $\hat y_A = 1$, so $y_A - \hat y_A = 0 - 1 = -1$ and
$\eta\,(y_A - \hat y_A) = 0.5 \times (-1) = -0.5$.
\begin{align*}
\vect{w}^{(1)} &= \vect{w}^{(0)} + \eta\,(y_A-\hat y_A)\,\vect{x}_A
               = (1.5,\,-0.5,\,2) - 0.5\,(3,\,4,\,2)\\
               &= (1.5,\,-0.5,\,2) - (1.5,\,2,\,1) = \finalans{(0,\,-2.5,\,1)}\\[4pt]
b^{(1)} &= b^{(0)} + \eta\,(y_A-\hat y_A) = -2 + (-0.5) = \finalans{-2.5}
\end{align*}

\question{E2.3}{1.5 marks}{Recompute scores and classes. What do you observe about a single update and about convergence?}
With $\vect{w}^{(1)} = (0,\,-2.5,\,1)$ and $b^{(1)} = -2.5$:
\begin{align*}
s_A^{(1)} &= (0)(3) + (-2.5)(4) + (1)(2) - 2.5 = 0 - 10 + 2 - 2.5 = -10.5\\
s_B^{(1)} &= (0)(1) + (-2.5)(5) + (1)(0) - 2.5 = 0 - 12.5 + 0 - 2.5 = -15\\
s_C^{(1)} &= (0)(2) + (-2.5)(6) + (1)(1) - 2.5 = 0 - 15 + 1 - 2.5 = -16.5
\end{align*}

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{lcccccl}\toprule
Loan & True $y$ & $s^{(0)}$ & $\hat y^{(0)}$ & $s^{(1)}$ & $\hat y^{(1)}$ & Change\\\midrule
A & 0 & $4.5$ & 1 & $-10.5$ & 0 & now correct\\
B & 1 & $-3$  & 0 & $-15$   & 0 & still wrong\\
C & 1 & $0$   & 1 & $-16.5$ & 0 & now wrong\\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}\end{table}

\textbf{Observation.} The update fixed loan A, but loan B is still wrong and loan C, which
was correct before, is now wrong. All three scores fell sharply because the update cut
$w_2$ from $-0.5$ to $-2.5$ and lowered $b$.

\textbf{Lesson.} One perceptron update only looks at one loan. It moves the boundary to help
that loan and can make other loans worse, so the number of errors can go up after an update.
The perceptron needs many passes over all the loans. It is guaranteed to stop only if the
data are linearly separable, and even then the path to that point is not smooth.

\subsection{Exercise 3: From score to probability}
Log-odds $z = -2 + 1.5\,x_1 - 0.5\,x_2$ and probability $p = \sigmoid(z) = \dfrac{1}{1+e^{-z}}$.
Given $e^{-1}=0.3679$, $e^{2}=7.3891$ and $e^{0}=1$.

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{lcc}\toprule
Borrower & $x_1$ (missed repayments) & $x_2$ (years in savings group)\\\midrule
P & 2 & 2\\ Q & 2 & 0\\ R & 0 & 0\\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}\end{table}

\question{E3.1}{2 marks}{Compute $z$ and $p$ for P, Q and R.}
\begin{align*}
z_P &= -2 + 1.5(2) - 0.5(2) = -2 + 3 - 1 = 0,
  & p_P &= \frac{1}{1+e^{0}} = \frac{1}{1+1} = \finalans{0.5000}\\[4pt]
z_Q &= -2 + 1.5(2) - 0.5(0) = -2 + 3 - 0 = 1,
  & p_Q &= \frac{1}{1+e^{-1}} = \frac{1}{1+0.3679} = \frac{1}{1.3679} = \finalans{0.7311}\\[4pt]
z_R &= -2 + 1.5(0) - 0.5(0) = -2,
  & p_R &= \frac{1}{1+e^{2}} = \frac{1}{1+7.3891} = \frac{1}{8.3891} = \finalans{0.1192}
\end{align*}

\question{E3.2}{1 mark}{Give the predicted class of each borrower at thresholds $0.5$ and $0.7$.}
Rule: predict default (1) if $p \geq t$, otherwise 0.

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{lccc}\toprule
Borrower & $p$ & $t = 0.5$ & $t = 0.7$\\\midrule
P & $0.5000$ & 1 \quad ($0.5000 \geq 0.5$) & 0 \quad ($0.5000 < 0.7$)\\
Q & $0.7311$ & 1 \quad ($0.7311 \geq 0.5$) & 1 \quad ($0.7311 \geq 0.7$)\\
R & $0.1192$ & 0 \quad ($0.1192 < 0.5$)    & 0 \quad ($0.1192 < 0.7$)\\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}\end{table}

Raising the threshold from 0.5 to 0.7 changes only P's prediction, from default to not default.

\question{E3.3}{1 mark}{Why does $z=0$ correspond to $p=0.5$, and what does $z=0$ mean for the decision boundary?}
\textbf{Why $p = 0.5$:} substituting $z = 0$ gives
\[
  p = \frac{1}{1+e^{-0}} = \frac{1}{1+1} = 0.5 .
\]
Since $z$ is the log-odds, $z = \ln\!\big(p/(1-p)\big) = 0$ means the odds are $1$, so default
and repayment are equally likely.

\textbf{Meaning for the boundary:} with the usual threshold 0.5, the points where $z = 0$ form
the decision boundary:
\[
  -2 + 1.5\,x_1 - 0.5\,x_2 = 0 .
\]
This is a straight line in the $(x_1, x_2)$ plane. Borrowers with $z > 0$ are predicted to
default and those with $z < 0$ are not. Borrower P has $z_P = 0$, so P lies exactly on this
boundary.

\subsection{Exercise 4: Metrics from a confusion matrix}
\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{lcc}\toprule
 & Predicted default & Predicted repaid\\\midrule
Actually defaulted & TP $=24$ & FN $=16$\\
Actually repaid    & FP $=12$ & TN $=148$\\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}\end{table}
Total $= 24 + 16 + 12 + 148 = 200$. Actual defaults $= 24 + 16 = 40$, actual repaid $= 12 + 148 = 160$.

\question{E4.1}{1 mark}{Accuracy and specificity.}
\begin{align*}
\text{Accuracy}    &= \frac{\text{TP}+\text{TN}}{\text{Total}} = \frac{24+148}{200} = \frac{172}{200} = \finalans{0.8600}\\[4pt]
\text{Specificity} &= \frac{\text{TN}}{\text{TN}+\text{FP}} = \frac{148}{148+12} = \frac{148}{160} = \finalans{0.9250}
\end{align*}

\question{E4.2}{1 mark}{Precision and recall.}
\begin{align*}
\text{Precision} &= \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP}+\text{FP}} = \frac{24}{24+12} = \frac{24}{36} = \finalans{0.6667}\\[4pt]
\text{Recall}    &= \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP}+\text{FN}} = \frac{24}{24+16} = \frac{24}{40} = \finalans{0.6000}
\end{align*}

\question{E4.3}{1 mark}{F1-score.}
\[
  F_1 = \frac{2 \times \text{Precision} \times \text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}}
      = \frac{2 \times 0.6667 \times 0.6000}{0.6667 + 0.6000}
      = \frac{0.8000}{1.2667} = \finalans{0.6316}
\]
Check with counts: $F_1 = \dfrac{2\,\text{TP}}{2\,\text{TP}+\text{FP}+\text{FN}} = \dfrac{48}{48+12+16} = \dfrac{48}{76} = 0.6316$.

\question{E4.4}{2 marks}{Accuracy of an always-``repaid'' baseline. Compare and interpret.}
The baseline predicts ``repaid'' for all 200 applicants, so it is right for the 160 who repaid:
\[
  \text{Baseline accuracy} = \frac{160}{200} = \finalans{0.8000},
  \qquad \text{Baseline recall} = \frac{0}{40} = 0 .
\]
The model's accuracy is $0.8600$, only $0.06$ higher than the baseline.

\textbf{What the comparison tells us:} the model does better than simply guessing the majority
class, so it has learned something from the features.

\textbf{What it does not tell us:} accuracy hides which errors are made. The baseline catches
none of the 40 defaulters, while the model catches 24 of them (recall 0.6000). Accuracy also
does not show the 16 defaulters the model still misses, which are the costly errors for a lender.

\question{E5.3}{1 mark}{What goes wrong if $x_2$ is in metres and $x_1$ in INR thousands? How does standardising help?}
In metres, a distance gap of 1 km becomes 1000, so its squared difference ($10^6$) swamps
income gaps of a few thousand rupees (squared differences around 4 to 16), and KNN would pick
neighbours almost only by distance to the branch. Standardising each feature,
$z = (x-\mu)/\sigma$, puts both on the same scale with mean 0 and standard deviation 1, so
income and distance count fairly in the distance.

\subsection{Exercise 6: Naive Bayes}
Ten past loans with yes/no features SG (savings-group member) and MP (missed an earlier payment).
New applicant: SG $=$ No and MP $=$ Yes. No smoothing.

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{cccc}\toprule
Loan & SG & MP & Default\\\midrule
1 & No & Yes & Yes\\ 2 & No & Yes & Yes\\ 3 & No & No & Yes\\ 4 & Yes & Yes & Yes\\
5 & Yes & No & No\\ 6 & Yes & No & No\\ 7 & Yes & Yes & No\\ 8 & No & No & No\\
9 & Yes & No & No\\ 10 & No & No & No\\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}\end{table}

\question{E6.1}{1.5 marks}{Prior probabilities and the class-conditional probabilities needed.}
\textbf{Priors.} Default $=$ Yes for loans 1 to 4 (4 loans) and Default $=$ No for loans 5 to 10 (6 loans):
\[
  P(\text{Yes}) = \frac{4}{10} = 0.4000, \qquad P(\text{No}) = \frac{6}{10} = 0.6000 .
\]
\textbf{Class-conditional probabilities.}

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{lcl}\toprule
Probability & Value & Loans counted\\\midrule
$P(\text{SG}=\text{No} \mid \text{Yes})$ & $3/4 = 0.7500$ & 1, 2, 3 out of 1 to 4\\
$P(\text{MP}=\text{Yes} \mid \text{Yes})$ & $3/4 = 0.7500$ & 1, 2, 4 out of 1 to 4\\
$P(\text{SG}=\text{No} \mid \text{No})$ & $2/6 = 0.3333$ & 8, 10 out of 5 to 10\\
$P(\text{MP}=\text{Yes} \mid \text{No})$ & $1/6 = 0.1667$ & 7 out of 5 to 10\\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}\end{table}

\question{E6.2}{1.5 marks}{Normalised posterior probability of default, and the predicted class.}
Let $X = (\text{SG}=\text{No},\ \text{MP}=\text{Yes})$. Unnormalised scores (prior $\times$ likelihoods):
\begin{align*}
P(\text{Yes})\,P(X \mid \text{Yes}) &= 0.4 \times \tfrac{3}{4} \times \tfrac{3}{4} = 0.4 \times 0.5625 = 0.2250\\[2pt]
P(\text{No})\,P(X \mid \text{No})   &= 0.6 \times \tfrac{2}{6} \times \tfrac{1}{6} = 0.6 \times 0.0556 = 0.0333
\end{align*}
Normalising by their sum, $0.2250 + 0.0333 = 0.2583$:
\begin{align*}
P(\text{Yes} \mid X) &= \frac{0.2250}{0.2583} = \finalans{0.8710}\\[4pt]
P(\text{No} \mid X)  &= \frac{0.0333}{0.2583} = 0.1290
\end{align*}
Since $0.8710 > 0.5$, the predicted class is \textbf{Default $=$ Yes}.

\question{E6.3}{1 mark}{State the independence assumption and one reason it may not hold here.}
\textbf{Assumption:} given the class, the features are independent:
\[
  P(\text{SG}, \text{MP} \mid \text{Default}) = P(\text{SG} \mid \text{Default}) \times P(\text{MP} \mid \text{Default}).
\]
\textbf{Why it may not hold:} savings-group membership and missed payments are likely to be
linked. Group members get peer support, regular saving habits and reminders from the group, so
they are less likely to miss a payment. Knowing SG then tells us something about MP even within
the same class, which breaks the assumption.
